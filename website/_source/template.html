Two steps, one answer

Most crowd apps show you a chart and leave the thinking to you. This one gives you a time, and the reason for it.

STEP 1

Pick a body group and a time

Arms, Legs, Cardio and four more. Then scroll the wheel to when you'd like to go, any half hour from 06:00 to 22:00.

STEP 2

Get the quietest time near it

The best time is circled and yours is crossed out. Every crowd is counted in tally marks. If moving would save less than half a person, it keeps your time.

AND THEN

Change your mind any time

Keep your own time, or tap Pick another time and the board re-checks the new one straight away. Switch days along the top.

It learns as it goes. Anyone can log how many people were really there. Those real counts replace the simulated ones, and the models retrain after every 10 reports, so the forecast gets closer to the real gym over time.

Two versions

Fit Forecaster started as a group college project. Version 2 is a rebuild of it: the same idea, but with honest scores, learning that works, and a real phone app.

Version 1 · group project

The first attempt

What it had

  • A notebook with a deep-learning model (an LSTM) trained on Tuesday data only.
  • A Python pipeline that simulated a month of visits from the members' timetable.
  • XGBoost, Random Forest and Linear Regression, averaged equally, plus Prophet and a neural network in the code.
  • A Streamlit dashboard with a crowd chart.

What was wrong

  • Its score (R² 0.91) was inflated: the models were tested on rows they had already learned from.
  • One clue, a rolling average, secretly included the answer.
  • Half hours with nobody there were missing, and "yesterday" meant the row above, not the day before.
  • Crowds that users reported were never used for learning, and fake member rows were written into the data files.
  • The dashboard showed a chart and left the decision to you.
Version 2 · rebuilt by Varshyan Suresh

The rebuild

What it has

  • Honest testing: learn from earlier days, get graded on later ones.
  • Clean data, and 13 clues that only look backwards.
  • A tuned team of three models for each body group.
  • Simulated members who behave more like people.
  • Learning from real reports that actually works.
  • One clear answer in two steps, instead of a chart.
  • A Flutter phone app on a FastAPI server, and this website.

What changed in version 2, and why

  1. Make the score honest

    What changed

    The models learn from the first 80% of days and are graded on the last 20%, which they never see while learning.

    Why

    Version 1 graded the models on rows they had already studied, like an exam where you've seen the answers.

    R² 0.91 → 0.78: the real starting point

  2. Fix the data

    What changed

    Half hours with nobody there now count as 0. "Yesterday" means the real day before. The rolling average stops before the day being guessed.

    Why

    The models never saw a quiet half hour, and one clue was quietly giving away the answer.

    A fair test, with no clue peeking

  3. Better models

    What changed

    XGBoost now knows it's counting people. Random Forest got stricter settings. A new clue was added: the usual crowd for that weekday. The team is weighted 45/45/10. Prophet was removed.

    Why

    To get as close as possible to the best score any model could reach on that data.

    R² 0.80, out of a best possible 0.81

  4. A more realistic gym

    What changed

    Simulated members now skip some days, often rest the day after training, and switch between their favourite workouts.

    Why

    Real people aren't that predictable. A harder, more realistic gym gives a score you can trust.

    R² 0.51, off by 1.9 people: lower, on purpose

  5. Learning that works

    What changed

    Reports are saved to a file and replace the simulated count for that half hour, and the models relearn after every 10 reports. The original data files are never touched.

    Why

    In version 1, reports were lost and never used for learning, so the app could never get better.

    Every real report brings it closer to the real gym

  6. One clear answer

    What changed

    The chart became two steps: pick a body group and a time, then get the quietest time within 90 minutes, with the reason. It keeps your time if moving saves less than half a person, and "Pick another time" lets you check a new one.

    Why

    At the gym you want an answer, not a chart to read. Getting there took seven design rounds (see Design below).

    When it moves you: about 2 fewer people

  7. A real app, and this site

    What changed

    A FastAPI server sends the forecasts to a Flutter phone app with a chalkboard look. This website and a plain-language explainer show how it all works.

    Why

    So it can be used on a phone, and so anyone can see how it works without reading the code.

    Try the demo, or read the explainer

The models

A small machine-learning pipeline in Python. Every score here comes from testing on days the models never saw. For a slower, clickable walk-through, see the models explained simply.

1

Data

Seven files, one per weekday: a timetable of 5,000 members, with when they usually arrive and their favourite workouts.

2

Simulated visits

There's no log of real visits, so a month of visits is simulated. Members turn up 85% of the time (70% the day after training) and switch between workouts.

3

13 clues

For each half hour: the time and day, yesterday's crowd, last week's, recent averages, and the usual crowd for that weekday. Only earlier days are used.

4

Three models

Each body group gets a team of three models. XGBoost and Random Forest get 45% of the say each, Linear Regression 10%.

Why these three models

45% OF THE SAY

XGBoost

What it does
Builds small decision trees one after another. Each new tree fixes some of the mistakes of the ones before it.
Why it's here
One of the two strongest here. It knows it's counting people, so it never guesses below zero and handles quiet half hours well.
Settings
300 small trees, each fixing only 5% of the mistake, so it doesn't overreact to one odd day.
1.88people off, on average, on its own
45% OF THE SAY

Random Forest

What it does
Grows 200 trees, each from a different random slice of the past, and averages their answers.
Why it's here
About as accurate as XGBoost, but it learns in a different way, so it makes different mistakes. Blending the two lets some of those mistakes cancel out.
Settings
200 trees. Every answer must come from at least 5 past examples, so it can't memorise one odd day.
1.88people off, on average, on its own
10% OF THE SAY

Linear Regression

What it does
Fits one straight-line rule: each clue pushes the guess up or down by a fixed amount.
Why it's here
The weakest alone, but simple and predictable. A small share keeps the blend grounded without dragging it down.
Settings
None to tune. It's the baseline the tree models have to beat.
2.02people off, on average, on its own

The final guess for each half hour is this weighted average. As a team they're off by 1.87 people on average, a little better than any one of them alone. The two tree models make different mistakes, so combining them cancels some out, and no single model decides your time on its own.

Why not just use the usual crowd for that time?

We tried it. A table of the usual crowd for each weekday and half hour is off by 1.95 people on average. The team is off by 1.87 and wins in 6 of the 7 body groups, because it also looks at yesterday, last week and recent days.

Why not deep learning (LSTM) or Prophet?

Both were in version 1. A month of half-hourly counts is very little history for models like these. Prophet made the results worse and was removed; the LSTM stayed an experiment. Tree models learn well from small tables like this one.

Why simulate visits?

The data is a weekly timetable of members, not a log of who came when. Simulating a month from it gives realistic day-to-day ups and downs, and every crowd people report in the app replaces a simulated one.

Why separate models for each body group?

Cardio, Legs and Arms fill up at different times and in different numbers. Separate models let each one learn its own pattern.

How well it reads each body group

R² is how much of the crowd's ups and downs the team explains: 1.0 is perfect, 0 is no better than always guessing the average. Busy groups have steadier patterns; small groups like Arms are mostly chance. Hover a bar to see how many people it's off by.

From dashboard to chalkboard

The answer has to read at a glance, on a phone, between sets. Getting there took a lot of rounds.

Each round cut something. The chart became one circled time, the list of reasons became one line, and the crowd became tally marks, the way a coach counts on the board.

Fonts went the same way. After sports, varsity, stencil and handwritten faces, the words went back to the phone's own font, and the chalk lives in the drawings: the frame, the circle, the marks.
