How it works · Fit Forecaster

How the app guesses how busy the gym will be

Explained simply, with the real numbers and things you can click. No maths needed.

The short version

  • The app looks at how busy the gym was on earlier days, and guesses how busy each half hour will be.
  • Three different "guessers" each make a guess. The app combines them into one.
  • Its guesses are off by about 1.9 people on average.
  • When it moves you to another time, you meet about 2.3 fewer people on average.

1The job

Say you want to train Legs at 19:30 on a Saturday. The app has to guess how many people will be doing Legs at 19:30, and at every half hour near it. Then it points you to the quietest one.

So the hard part is the guessing. The rest of this page is about how the guessing works, and how good it is.

You just did what the app does: you looked at the clues and made a sensible guess. The difference is that the app has learned from hundreds of past examples how much to trust each clue.

Fancy name: the clues are called features. The app uses 13 of them; the full list is at the bottom of the page.

3Learning from the past (Guesser 1)

Take one clue: how busy that time usually is on that weekday. Every dot below is one half hour from the past. Left to right is the usual crowd. Bottom to top is how many people really came.

There's a pattern (usually busy means probably busy) but the dots are spread out. People are random. The app's job is to find the pattern under the noise.

The straight line: Guesser 1

Draw one line through the middle of the dots. To guess, go up from the usual crowd to the line. Simple and steady, but the real pattern isn't always straight.

Fancy name: Linear Regression.

The staircase: the idea behind Guessers 2 and 3

Split the dots into groups (quiet, medium, busy, very busy) and use each group's average. It can bend to fit the data, which straight lines can't.

Fancy name: a decision tree.

The real app does this with all 13 clues at once, not just one. That's hard to draw, but the idea is exactly the same.

4The tree of questions

The staircase is really a decision tree, and the next two guessers are built from lots of them. A decision tree is a game of yes/no questions, like "20 Questions". Each answer sends you left or right, until you reach an answer at the bottom.

Below is a real tree the app learned from the Legs data. Click through it with our Saturday 19:30.

The real trees ask many more questions, so their answers are much finer than these 4 boxes. But one tree on its own is a bit jumpy: small changes in the data can change its questions. So the app doesn't trust just one.

5Guesser 2: ask 200 trees

Think of guessing the number of sweets in a jar. One person's guess can be way off. The average of 200 people's guesses is usually close. That's this guesser.

It grows 200 trees, each from a slightly different random slice of the past, so each one guesses differently. Then it takes the average. These are the real guesses of all 200 trees for our evening:

Fancy name: Random Forest.

6Guesser 3: fix the mistakes, step by step

This one works like a student going through practice tests. Start with a rough guess. Check where it was wrong on past days. Add a small tree that fixes a bit of that mistake. Repeat, 300 times.

Each fix is small on purpose (only 5% of a full correction), so it doesn't overreact to one strange day. Drag the slider to watch its guess for our evening build up:

Fancy name: XGBoost (gradient boosting). It's also told the answer is a count of people, so it never guesses below zero.

7The team vote

The app asks all three guessers, then combines them. The two tree guessers are the strongest, so each gets 45% of the say. The straight line is weaker, so it gets 10%: enough to steady things, not enough to drag them down.

Why bother with a team? Different guessers make different mistakes. When you average them, some mistakes cancel out. The team ends up a little more accurate than any one guesser (section 9 shows the numbers).

10Does it actually help you?

Good guesses don't automatically mean good advice, so we tested the advice itself. On every exam day, for every body group and every time you could pick, we let the app suggest a time, then checked the real crowd.

11Where it struggles

  • Small crowds are hard. When 2 to 4 people is normal (Arms, Core), one person more or less is a big change, and nobody can predict that.
  • The gym visits are simulated. The project has a weekly timetable of members, not a log of real visits, so a month of visits was simulated from it. Real check-in data would help the most.
  • A month is short. That's only 4 or 5 of each weekday to learn from.
  • It doesn't know about holidays, weather or exam weeks.
  • It gets better with use. When people log how busy it really was, those real counts replace the simulated ones, and the app relearns after every 10 reports.

Which clues matter most

How much each clue helped the tree guessers, averaged over all body groups. Recent history and the time of day do most of the work.

Scores for every body group, including R²

R² is the score data scientists usually quote. It says how much of the ups and downs the guess explains: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than always guessing the average. The app's average R² is 0.51.

How the score was fixed (it used to say 0.91)

The original version of this project reported R² 0.91, but two bugs inflated it: the exam questions were also in the study material, and one clue secretly included the answer. After fixing both, on the first, simpler simulation, the score was 0.80 out of a best possible 0.81. The simulation was then made more realistic (members skip days, rest after training, change workouts), which makes crowds more random, so today's scores are lower but closer to a real gym.
